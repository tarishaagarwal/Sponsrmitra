# SponsrMitra: Job Spec

A fest team hires SponsrMitra to turn a blank sponsor list into confirmed sponsorship money, without months of cold outreach, guesswork about what to ask for, or sponsors who vanish before signing. Figures and timings below are working assumptions to test, not measured data.

## Who needs it

The primary user is the student who owns sponsorship for a college fest. Three other groups decide whether that person succeeds: the rest of the fest team, the brands on the other side of the deal, and the alumni who can open doors.

### Primary user: the sponsorship lead (fest side)

This is usually a second- or third-year student, aged 19 to 22, who volunteers to run sponsorship, often for the first time. They sit inside a core committee with a convenor, a finance or treasury head and a faculty advisor. They carry a deadline and a rupee target, and they inherit almost no institutional memory: last year's lead has graduated, and the contacts live in that person's phone and WhatsApp chats.

What defines them:

- They are accountable for a target they did not set and cannot easily estimate.
- They work on sponsorship alongside classes, internships and placement preparation, so hours are scarce.
- They have no sales training and no template for what a sponsor expects to see.
- They are judged in public: the scale of the fest, its headline acts and its prize pool depend on the money they bring in.

### Secondary users on the fest side

- **Convenor.** Needs a weekly view of confirmed money against the target so spending decisions on artists, venue and prizes can be made.
- **Finance or treasury head.** Needs sponsor commitments, payment dates and invoices in one place.
- **Sponsorship team members (typically 3 to 8 volunteers).** Need one shared list so two people do not email the same company with different asks.
- **Faculty advisor or student affairs office.** Needs assurance that outreach uses the institution's name responsibly.

### The other side: brand and marketing teams

Brand-side users are campus marketing managers, youth-marketing leads, campus-hiring teams and, in smaller firms, the founder. They receive many unsolicited fest decks each season, most of them incomplete or near-identical. They need to find fests that match their audience and budget, and to trust that the fest is real and will deliver what it promises: visibility, footfall, leads, sampling or a hiring pipeline.

### Supporting users: alumni

Alumni at target companies are the bridge for warm introductions. They are not customers, but the product fails if they do not respond. An intro request must be short, specific and cost them under two minutes to act on.

### Who this is not for at launch

- Large professional events such as concerts and expos, which generalist marketplaces already serve.
- School-level events, where sponsor budgets and brand interest differ sharply.
- Colleges outside India.

### Segments by type of fest

| Fest type | Typical sponsors | Why they need help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical and hackathon | Tech firms, developer tools, recruiters | Sponsors want hiring access; students lack pricing benchmarks |
| Cultural | FMCG, beverages, apparel, telecom, media | Crowded pitches; sponsors want proof of footfall |
| Management and business | Banks, consulting, fintech, ed-tech | Sponsors want a professional audience |
| Sports | Sportswear, energy drinks, fitness brands | Deals are often in kind (kit, equipment, refreshments) |
| Society and department events | Local businesses, cafes, print shops | Small cheques or in-kind support that large platforms ignore |

The last row is the deliberate wedge: small, local and in-kind deals are high in volume and low in glamour, so generalist platforms tend to under-serve them.

## When they need it

A fest team needs SponsrMitra when the fest is first being planned, when outreach stalls, and when a deal is agreed but not yet signed or paid. Brands need it earlier than students expect, because campus budgets tend to be set well before fest season.

### Trigger moments on the fest side

- **The committee is formed.** A new lead asks who was approached last year and what they paid. Nobody can answer. This is the strongest moment to start.
- **The target is set.** The convenor announces a number, and the lead must learn within days whether it is realistic.
- **The first 20 emails go unanswered.** Morale drops and the team looks for another route. Alumni introductions become attractive.
- **A sponsor says maybe.** A company asks for a deck, a footfall estimate or past sponsors, and the team scrambles.
- **A deal is agreed verbally.** The team needs a written commitment, deliverables and a payment schedule before interest cools.
- **The fest ends.** Sponsors expect a report. A team that sends one earns a renewal; one that does not usually loses the sponsor.

### A typical sponsorship calendar (assumption)

Timings vary by college, so the table counts back from the fest. Validate it with 10 to 15 fest leads.

| Time before fest | What the team is doing | What they need from SponsrMitra |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6 to 5 months | Forming the team, setting a target, inheriting contacts | Benchmarks for a realistic target; a shortlist by category |
| 5 to 4 months | Building the deck, mapping companies, finding names | Verified contacts, a deck template, alumni intro requests |
| 4 to 2 months | Outreach, follow-ups, meetings, negotiation | Pipeline tracker, scheduling, follow-up reminders |
| 2 months to fest | Closing, signing, collecting first payments | Commitment records, deliverables checklist |
| Fest week | Delivering branding, stalls and mentions | Deliverables checklist shared with the sponsor |
| 1 month after | Reporting to sponsors, handing over | Sponsor report template; archive for the next team |

### When brands need it

Brand budgets are usually planned before the year they are spent. Many Indian companies plan on an April to March financial year, so campus and youth-marketing spend is often allocated in the first quarter or two, well before most fests ask. A brand therefore wants to see fests early, compare them and reserve budget. Requests that arrive a month or two before a fest are far more likely to be declined for lack of budget or approval time.

Brands also have pressure points: a product launch that needs student sampling, a hiring drive that needs campus visibility, or a competitor already active on a campus. In those moments a verified, well-matched fest listing is worth more than a cold deck.

### Timing constraints the product must respect

- **Approval lag.** Corporate decisions can take weeks. Show where each request sits, so students do not read silence as rejection.
- **Exam and placement seasons.** Volunteers disappear at predictable times. A shared pipeline lets others pick up the work.
- **The handover cliff.** The team changes every year. Contacts and history must stay with the fest.
- **Deadline pressure.** In the final weeks, teams accept weak deals. Early benchmarks help them refuse bad terms.

### Why timing shapes the product

The largest value comes early. The six months before the fest are when a good shortlist and a realistic target change the outcome. A tool that only helps in the final month arrives after the important decisions are made. That is why the first release focuses on discovery, benchmarks and warm introductions rather than payment processing.

## The jobs

### The core job

"When I have been handed a sponsorship target and a few months to hit it, I want to find the right companies, approach them in a way that gets answered, and close deals on fair terms, so I can fund the fest without burning out my team or embarrassing my college."

### Functional jobs on the fest side

1. **Decide how much to ask for.** Set a realistic target and a per-sponsor ask based on fest size, type and sponsor category.
2. **Find the right companies.** Shortlist companies that sponsor fests like this one.
3. **Reach a real person.** Get a verified contact with authority over sponsorship, not a generic inbox.
4. **Get a warm introduction.** Ask an alumnus at the company to vouch for the fest instead of sending a cold message.
5. **Make the pitch credible.** Produce a deck and footfall evidence that answers what sponsors ask.
6. **Manage the conversation.** Track who was contacted, what was said, the next step and the owner.
7. **Close and document.** Turn a verbal yes into a written commitment with deliverables and payment dates.
8. **Deliver and report.** Track deliverables, then send a report that earns a renewal.
9. **Hand over.** Leave the next team an archive of contacts, terms and outcomes.

### Functional jobs on the brand side

- Discover fests that match the brand's audience, city and budget.
- Verify that a fest is genuine and its numbers are plausible.
- Compare offers from different fests like for like.
- Get proof of delivery to justify the spend internally.

### Emotional jobs

- **Feel in control instead of guessing.** Students want to know their ask is fair and their list is sensible.
- **Feel confident in the room.** Meeting a corporate manager is intimidating; preparation reduces that.
- **Stop dreading the inbox.** Silence is stressful when nobody can tell whether it means no, maybe or not yet.
- **Trust the other side.** Both parties want to know the other is who they claim to be.

### Social jobs

- Be seen by the committee, seniors and faculty as competent and reliable.
- Build a network of alumni and company contacts that outlasts the fest, and show measurable results on a CV.
- For the college: protect its name with companies that will be approached again next year.
- For brands: be seen as a supporter of students and, for hiring teams, as an employer worth knowing.

### Job statements by user

| User | When... | I want to... | So I can... |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sponsorship lead | I inherit a target with no past data | see what similar fests raised and from whom | set a realistic target and ask |
| Sponsorship lead | my cold emails get no reply | ask an alumnus at the company to introduce me | get a first meeting |
| Team member | several of us contact companies | see who has been approached and by whom | avoid duplicate outreach |
| Convenor | I must approve artist and venue spend | see confirmed, likely and hoped-for money separately | commit spend without risking a shortfall |
| Finance head | a sponsor agrees | record the amount, terms and payment dates | chase payments on time |
| Brand manager | I have a campus budget to place | compare verified fests by audience, city and offer | reach the right students |
| Alumnus | a junior asks for an introduction | forward a short, clear request | help without giving up an evening |

### What the product must not do

- **Charge students for basic information.** Fest teams have almost no budget, so revenue has to come from the brand side or from optional extras.
- **Spam brands.** Unvetted, repetitive requests would destroy the trust that makes brands respond.
- **Expose contact details without consent.** Names and emails are personal data. Collect consent and follow applicable data-protection law.

## What they should be able to accomplish

By the end of one fest cycle, a first-time sponsorship lead should be able to do everything below without outside help. Each outcome has a test that can be observed in a user session. The time targets are hypotheses to check with real fest teams.

### Fest-side outcomes

| # | Outcome | Acceptance test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Set a realistic target | Within 10 minutes of signing up, a lead enters fest type, size and city and sees the range comparable fests raised, split into cash and in-kind |
| 2 | Build a shortlist | In under 30 minutes, a lead lists at least 20 verified, relevant companies, filtered by category, region and deal type |
| 3 | Reach a decision-maker | Every shortlisted company shows a named contact, role, verification date and preferred channel |
| 4 | Request a warm introduction | A lead sends an alumni intro request in under 2 minutes and can see whether it was seen, accepted or declined |
| 5 | Send a credible pitch | A lead builds a deck from a template using their own fest data and attaches past-sponsor and footfall evidence |
| 6 | Run the pipeline as a team | Every member sees the same list, statuses and owners; contacting a company someone else already approached triggers a warning |
| 7 | Close on paper | A lead records a commitment with amount, deliverables and payment dates, visible to the convenor and finance head |
| 8 | Deliver and report | A lead ticks off deliverables and sends a sponsor report within a week of the fest |
| 9 | Hand over | A new lead opens last year's archive on day one and sees every company approached and the outcome |

### Brand-side outcomes

| # | Outcome | Acceptance test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Find matching fests | A manager filters verified fests by city, audience, size, dates and format, and has a shortlist in under 10 minutes |
| 2 | Trust the listing | Each fest shows verification status, past-sponsor references and evidence for its footfall claims |
| 3 | Respond easily | A manager can accept, decline or ask a question in one click, and decline reasons flow back to the fest |
| 4 | Compare like for like | Offers appear in one format: benefits, deliverables, price and audience |
| 5 | Get proof | After the event the manager receives a delivery report they can forward internally |

### Alumni outcome

An alumnus can review an intro request in under two minutes and answer with one tap: introduce, decline, or suggest someone else.

### What "done" looks like for a fest cycle

- We know how much we raised, from whom, and what is still pending.
- No sponsor was contacted twice by different people.
- Every confirmed sponsor has a written record of what they were promised.
- Every sponsor received a report after the event.
- The next team can start from our archive, not from zero.

### Stretch outcomes after launch

- Renew a past sponsor for next year in one click from the saved record.
- Bundle several fests from one college into a combined offer for a brand.
- Suggest sponsors automatically from what similar fests have closed.

## What they use today, and why they would switch

Fest teams already hire several things for this job. The descriptions below are working assumptions, not research, and should be confirmed in interviews.

| Current solution | What it does well | Where it fails | What would make them switch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cold email and LinkedIn | Free, fast, no gatekeeper | Low reply rates, outdated names, no view of status | Verified contacts plus a warm-intro route |
| Seniors' contacts and WhatsApp groups | Warm and trusted | Depend on one person, lost at handover | A shared archive that stays with the fest |
| Team spreadsheets | Flexible and familiar | Go stale, no reminders, no benchmarks | A pipeline as easy as a sheet, with owners and status |
| Generalist marketplaces such as Onspon | Wide brand reach | Built for professional event managers; little guidance for first-time students; no fest benchmarks | A student-friendly workflow, fest benchmarks and in-kind deals |
| Agencies and consultants | Experience and relationships | Take a commission, favour large fests | Low-cost tooling for teams that cannot pay a commission |
| Alumni cells and placement offices | Real access to companies | Slow, not built for sponsorship | A structured intro request alumni can answer in minutes |

**Why students will switch.** In order of importance: more replies, time saved, confidence in the ask, and one shared view for the team. If reply rates do not rise, nothing else matters.

**Why brands will switch.** They already have a way to say no: they ignore unsolicited decks. They will use SponsrMitra only if it reduces noise through verified fests, a standard listing format and relevant matches.

### Habits and switching costs to overcome

- **WhatsApp is the default.** Teams coordinate there already, so alerts should go to WhatsApp and email instead of demanding daily logins.
- **Free is the expectation.** Students will not pay, so the product must be free on the fest side.
- **Importing old data is a chore.** Let teams upload their existing spreadsheet on day one, so the product starts useful.

### Where SponsrMitra should not compete

It should not try to match the breadth of generalist marketplaces or list concerts, expos and corporate events. Its advantage is depth on one narrow job: college fest sponsorship, done well for first-time student teams.

## Requirements

"Must" means the first release fails without it; "Should" can follow within the first season; "Later" is out of scope for now.

| Feature | Job it serves | Priority | Requirement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Verified company directory | Find companies; reach a real person | Must | Category, sponsorship types, region, named contact, role and last-verified date; unverified entries never appear |
| Benchmark data | Decide how much to ask | Must | Anonymised deal ranges by fest size, type and sponsor category, cash and in-kind, shown only above a minimum sample size |
| Alumni warm-intro requests | Get a warm introduction | Must | College-tagged alumni, a pre-filled request, status tracking, one-tap response |
| Shared pipeline | Manage the conversation | Must | Statuses, owners, next steps, reminders; duplicate-outreach warning; CSV import |
| Fest profile and verification | Trust on both sides | Must | Dates, size, audience and past sponsors; verified through a college email or faculty confirmation |
| Handover archive | Hand over | Should | Contacts, terms and outcomes stay with the fest for the next team |
| Deck and email templates | Make the pitch credible | Should | Templates filled from the fest profile, plus a checklist of what sponsors ask |
| Meeting scheduling | Manage the conversation | Should | A calendar link inside each request, with reminders |
| Commitment record | Close and document | Should | Amount, deliverables and payment dates, visible to the convenor and finance head |
| Sponsor report | Deliver and report | Should | Deliverables checklist shared with the sponsor and a report template |
| Brand dashboard | Brand jobs | Should | Filters, shortlist, one-click accept or decline, comparison view |
| Payment processing | Close and document | Later | Record payment dates only; do not move money in the first release |

### Quality requirements

- **Verification.** A human checks every contact, shows the check date, and removes a contact after a bounce or a wrong-number report. Re-check at least every 90 days.
- **Mobile first.** Every screen works at 360 px width on a slow connection.
- **Notifications.** Alerts go to email and WhatsApp, so nobody needs to log in daily.
- **Privacy.** Collect consent for contact details, allow deletion on request, and limit views by role.
- **Anti-spam.** Cap the number of new requests a fest can send per week, and let brands pause inbound requests.

## Success measures

The north-star measure is confirmed sponsorship value, in rupees and in-kind, closed per fest through SponsrMitra in one season. Targets marked "baseline" should be set after collecting each team's numbers from the previous year.

| Measure | What it tells us | Working target | How to measure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmed sponsorship value per fest | Whether the job is getting done | Set after baseline | Commitment records |
| Outreach reply rate | Whether verified contacts beat cold email | Clearly above the team's own baseline | Replies divided by messages sent |
| Alumni intro acceptance | Whether the warm route works | Set after the first 50 requests | Accepted intros per 100 requests |
| Time to first shortlist | Whether discovery is fast | Under 30 minutes | Session timing from sign-up |
| Duplicate outreach incidents | Whether teams coordinate | Zero | Warnings triggered and overridden |
| Brand response rate | Whether requests are worth a brand's time | Most answered within 14 days | Responses divided by requests received |
| Contact accuracy | Whether verification holds up | Every contact re-checked within 90 days | Share of contacts with a recent check |
| Returning fests | Whether teams find lasting value | Most return the next season | Fests active in two consecutive seasons |

### Signs the product is failing

- Teams sign up but never import contacts or send a request.
- Brands ignore most inbound requests, so listings are not relevant enough.
- Alumni let intro requests sit unanswered for more than a week.
- Verifying each contact costs more than the product can recover.

### How to read the numbers

Lead with reply rate and intro acceptance in the first season, because they show whether the core promise holds. Sponsorship value follows later and depends on factors outside the product, such as a fest's reputation and the economy. Report all figures per fest, not in aggregate, so one large fest cannot hide the results of many small ones.

## Risks and assumptions to test before building

The main risk is not building the app. It is getting both sides of the marketplace to show up. The stop signals are working thresholds to adjust once real numbers arrive.

| Assumption or risk | How to test | Stop signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brands and fests will each wait for the other | Recruit 25 to 30 companies by hand and serve 3 to 5 fests through a group chat and a shared sheet | Fewer than 10 companies confirm interest |
| Brands will confirm they sponsor fests and share a contact | Ask 30 brand contacts for a yes or no and a preferred channel | Most decline or never reply |
| Alumni respond to intro requests | Send 20 requests from one college and track answers within 7 days | Fewer than a quarter are answered |
| Benchmark data can be collected | Offer fests benchmarks in exchange for their own deal figures; start with 30 fests | No category reaches a usable sample after one season |
| Verification is affordable | Time 50 verifications and estimate the yearly cost per contact | Cost exceeds what brands will pay |
| Brands will pay | Offer a paid listing or success fee to 10 brands | No brand agrees to pay in any form |
| Fraud and impersonation | Verify fests through a college email and faculty confirmation; add a report button | A fraud case reaches a live brand contact |
| Students graduate every year | Build ties with student affairs offices so each college has a standing owner | Fewer returning fests than new ones after two seasons |
| Competitors extend into college fests | Review competitors each quarter; focus on benchmarks and alumni intros | A competitor launches both for free |

## Four-week validation plan

Run this before relying on the software, using a group chat and a spreadsheet.

| Week | What we do | What we learn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Interview 10 to 15 fest leads and 8 to 10 brand contacts; collect last year's outreach and reply numbers | The real calendar, pain points and a baseline reply rate |
| 2 | Build hand-made shortlists for 3 fests from 25 to 30 verified companies | Whether brands confirm interest and a contact |
| 3 | Send alumni intro requests and pitches through a shared sheet and a group chat | Reply and acceptance rates against each team's baseline |
| 4 | Fill in the success measures and ask brands about paying | Whether to go ahead, adjust or stop |

- **Go** if the reply rate clearly beats the baseline for at least two of the three fests, at least 10 companies confirm a contact, and at least one brand would pay.
- **Adjust** if replies improve but brands will not confirm contacts, or brands respond but alumni do not. Change the weaker half of the offer and repeat for two weeks.
- **Stop** if neither replies nor intros beat cold email, or if verifying a contact takes too long to scale.

### Interview questions for week 1

**Fest sponsorship leads**

1. Walk me through the last time you tried to get a sponsor. What did you do first?
2. How many companies did you contact, and how many replied?
3. Where did you find contact names, and how often were they wrong?
4. How did you decide how much to ask for?
5. What happened to last year's contacts when the previous team left?
6. What would you pay for, if anything, and who would approve it?

**Brand contacts**

1. How many college sponsorship requests do you receive in a season?
2. What makes you answer one and ignore the rest?
3. When is your campus budget set, and who approves spending?
4. What proof do you need from a fest before saying yes?
5. Would you confirm your interest and a contact name for a verified directory, and what would you want in return?

**Alumni**

1. Would you introduce a junior to your company's marketing team, and what would make that easy?

## Open questions

- Which city or cluster of colleges hosts the pilot?
- Who verifies contacts, and what does each check cost?
- Should benchmark data be public, or visible only to fests that contribute their own figures?
- Which brand categories join first?
- What must a college's student affairs office approve before fests can use the product?
